A Bus That Runs Is Not Always a Bus You Can Rely On.

AC Transit delivered most scheduled trips through summer 2026. But reliability looks different from the rider’s stop—and the first complete days after the fall schedule shift show why that distinction matters.

Schedule-shift dates are data gaps

A schedule-shift date is the first service date covered by a new GTFS version. On June 14 and August 9, the prior feed had expired the day before, while this tracker did not archive the newly effective feed until 10:00 p.m. PT. Each date therefore contains only the final hours of tracked service and produces an artificial trip-delivery result—7.1% on June 14 and 6.2% on August 9. Both dates are excluded. Post-shift in this report means the nine complete days after the fall change, August 10–18.

The rider experience

A bus can run and still fail the rider

Across the study, AC Transit delivered 90.2% of 433,746 scheduled trips. But only 73.4% of scheduled stop rows on observed trips produced an arrival from one minute early through seven minutes late. Reliability depends on both.

A trip can appear to have run in the data and still be unusable from a rider’s perspective. After repeated frequency cuts over the past decade, every missed or no-show bus carries a larger penalty: riders must plan work, medical appointments, and other commitments around scheduled arrivals, and one failure can unravel the whole trip.

What the data supports

The strongest conclusion is not that summer caused a systemwide reliability decline. Local and Transbay performance was broadly stable through the summer schedule. The preliminary post-shift period is the period that deserves attention.

Finding 01

Summer was mostly steady

Local stop delivery was 73.9% both before and during summer. Transbay improved from 65.0% to 66.3%, while strict on-time performance rose slightly in both groups.

Finding 02

Post-shift service softened at stops

Across the system, stop delivery fell from 73.6% in the baseline to 71.5% after the fall schedule shift. Transbay fell 4.4 points and All Nighter fell 4.7 points, even though a larger share of their scheduled trips ran.

Finding 03

School trips returned, but late

School-route trip delivery returned near baseline: 93.0% versus 94.3%. Stop delivery improved, but strict on-time arrivals slipped to 34.0% and arrivals over seven minutes late rose to 14.4%.

Why deduplication matters

The live tracker can finalize the same trip more than once. Every query keeps one row per service date, trip, and stop, preferring a row with an actual arrival and then the latest ingestion. Without this step, counts and percentages would be biased toward repeatedly finalized trips.

Concern 1: Did the bus run?

Trip delivery compares the GTFS schedule with trips that produced at least one actual stop arrival. Across the full study, 90.2% of 433,746 scheduled trips were observed.

This is the most generous reliability test: a trip counts as having run after one observed arrival. It is necessary, but not sufficient, for rider reliability. A bus can register as running and still miss stops, arrive outside a useful window, or end early.

Concern 2: Did it serve the stop usefully?

A stop is counted as delivered when an actual arrival is observed from one minute early through seven minutes late. The denominator includes every scheduled stop on observed trips, including stops the bus never reached.

The divergence between trips and stops is the most important result. All Nighter trip delivery improved by 5.9 points after the fall shift, but stop delivery declined by 4.7 points. Transbay shows the same direction: more scheduled trips were observed, yet fewer scheduled stops were reached within the useful window.

Summer performance

Summer was broadly stable

The eight-week summer window does not support a blanket claim that service became less reliable. Local stop delivery held at 73.9%, while Transbay rose from 65.0% to 66.3%. Strict on-time performance also rose slightly in both groups.

Stability is not the same as strong performance. The result says that summer did not produce a broad deterioration against the pre-summer baseline; it does not say that a 73.9% useful-stop result is good enough.

After the fall schedule shift

The first complete post-shift days deserve attention

Across August 10–18, systemwide stop delivery fell to 71.5%. Transbay fell 4.4 points from baseline and All Nighter fell 4.7 points, even as their scheduled-trip delivery improved. Nine complete days are a warning signal to monitor, not proof that the schedule change caused the decline.

Read the post-shift period as preliminary. It contains nine complete service days, August 10–18. The changes are signals to monitor, not evidence that the schedule change caused them.

The waiting burden is the most severe rider pain point—not completely dropped buses

Completely dropped or severely truncated trips are uncommon in these windows. The more persistent burden is time: post-shift waits averaged 18.8 minutes on Local service, 19.2 minutes on Transbay service, and 26.7 minutes on All Nighter service. Because a bus can appear to miss its final stop when its last GPS point falls just short of the terminal, this report counts a trip as severely truncated only when no arrival is observed beyond 80% of its scheduled stop sequence.

Waits changed little across the three windows. That matters: the preliminary post-shift decline is mostly a stop-delivery and punctuality story, not evidence of a systemwide headway collapse.

School service

School service returned near baseline—with more lateness

AC Transit suspended lines 600–699 for summer and resumed them on August 9. Comparing their active pre-summer days with seven complete post-shift school-service days shows a near-baseline return in trip delivery and severe truncation, but a less punctual service.

School 600–699

Trip delivery was 93.0%, versus 94.3% before summer. Stop delivery improved from 63.6% to 65.4%. But on-time arrivals fell two points, while the share over seven minutes late rose 3.7 points. Severe truncation was also near baseline: 3.3% post-shift versus 3.5% before summer.

Calendar caveat

School routes do not all operate on every weekday. The comparison uses only service that appears in each date’s GTFS schedule; it does not assume every 600-series route should run daily.

A public reliability scorecard

Measure reliability at the stop

Publishing whether scheduled trips ran is a useful start. A rider-centered scorecard should also show whether those trips reached their scheduled stops, when they arrived, whether they completed most of the route, and how irregular service changed the wait.

Scheduled-trip deliveryDid the service in the timetable appear?
Stop-level deliveryDid the trip reach each stop inside a rider-useful window?
Early and late sharesHow often did an arrival miss the promise in either direction?
Severe truncationHow often did a trip fail to reach the final fifth of its stop sequence?
Headway-based waitsWhat wait did irregular spacing impose on a random-arrival rider?

These measures should be published routinely by service family and by route. The summer result shows why: a stable system average can coexist with weak stop-level performance, and a post-shift system signal can hide routes moving sharply in opposite directions.

Claims the data supports

  • Summer was broadly stable.
  • The preliminary post-shift period deserves monitoring.
  • Trip delivery alone overstates the rider experience.
  • School service returned near baseline but with more very-late arrivals.

Claims to avoid

  • That the August schedule change caused the declines.
  • That Early Bird is definitively the least reliable family.
  • That every trip missing its final observed stop was cut short.
  • That these route-weighted results equal rider-weighted experience.

Methodology & sources

How to reproduce and interpret the analysis

Definitions

  • Trip delivery: observed trips ÷ GTFS-scheduled trips.
  • Stop delivery: actual arrival from −1 to +7 minutes ÷ all scheduled stops on observed trips.
  • Strictly on time: actual arrival from scheduled time through +3 minutes.
  • Very late: more than 7 minutes after schedule.
  • Severely truncated: no arrival observed beyond 80% of the scheduled stop sequence.
  • Mean rider wait: random-arrival wait estimated from actual 1–120 minute headways.

Route groups

  • Early Bird: 701, 702, 703.
  • All Nighter: 800, 801, 802, 805, 840, 851.
  • School: numeric routes 600–699.
  • Transbay: E, F, FS, G, J, L, NL, NX, NX3, O, P, U, V, W, excluding the special groups above.
  • Local / other: all remaining routes.

Limitations

  • The post-shift period is only nine complete days, so it is preliminary and not causal evidence.
  • June 14 and August 9 are excluded pipeline-coverage gaps, not evidence that transit service collapsed on those dates.
  • Metrics weight stop observations, trips, or route-stop headways—not passenger boardings. High-ridership routes do not receive extra weight.
  • Final-terminal GPS behavior makes “any incomplete trip” too noisy for a headline; the 80% threshold is used instead.
  • Early Bird results have very small stop and headway samples and unusual service-day behavior.
  • The archived two-consecutive-missed-bus metric begins July 12 and is not used for baseline comparisons.
