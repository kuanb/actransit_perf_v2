Where AC Transit service disappears for 40 minutes at a time

Four days of actual-arrival data show repeated holes on several of the district’s busiest lines. This is where I would go, and when, to talk to riders experiencing them.

I have been spending a lot of time lately trying to understand the difference between a bus route that looks acceptable as an average and one that is actually useful when a rider walks up to a stop. A line can run most of its scheduled trips over a full day and still produce a 45- or 60-minute hole at exactly the time someone needs it.

So, I asked a practical question: if a reporter wanted to stand on the street and talk to riders while one of these failures was happening, where should they go? Using data from the AC Transit Report Card, I looked at actual arrivals from Monday, August 24 through Thursday, August 27 at central stops on AC Transit’s seven busiest routes. Arrivals were separated by route, stop, and direction; a bus going the other way cannot close the gap.

A dash does not mean the line ran well all day; it means that this particular stop and direction test did not produce a gap strictly greater than 40 minutes. The Monday 1T result is a good example of how arbitrary a hard threshold can be: its 7:57–8:36 PM gap was 39.5 minutes, missing the cutoff by about 30 seconds.

Two more busy routes are worth watching

Line 52, ninth by scheduled trips, had 16 qualifying gaps at North Berkeley BART on three of four days. The worst was Wednesday toward UC Berkeley: 9:07–11:35 PM, or 148.1 minutes, with five scheduled arrivals inside the gap.

Line 97, tenth by scheduled trips, had 14 qualifying gaps at Union Landing Transit Center across all four days. Its worst was Monday toward Union City BART: 7:06–8:13 AM, or 67.1 minutes, with four scheduled arrivals inside.

Line 18, eighth by scheduled trips, had two qualifying gaps at Uptown Transit Center, both Tuesday afternoon toward Montclair. They lasted 42.2 and 44.0 minutes and each contained three scheduled arrivals.

Where I would send a reporter

I tested fixed two-hour observation windows. A “hit” means that the visit would have overlapped at least 30 minutes of a direction-specific gap longer than 40 minutes. This is useful for deciding where to interview riders, but it is still just four days of history.

Those would be my first four choices. Lower-confidence options are the 51A at 12th Street BART from 9–11 PM and the 51B at Downtown Berkeley BART from 7:30–9:30 PM; each hit on only one of four days. Line 9 failed on all four days, but at wildly different times, so there is no honest fixed interview window to recommend from this sample. I would not send someone to Line 57 at MacArthur BART based on these four days.

The most striking result is not simply that a bus was late. It is that on some of the East Bay’s busiest lines, multiple scheduled buses can fit inside the space between two actual arrivals. For the 1T and 40, this was not an isolated middle-of-the-night event. It was a recurring evening pattern at stops where people should be able to depend on frequent service. This is really bad service, folks!

Methodology and an important caveat

I ranked routes using scheduled trips from August 24–27, 2026, then selected a central stop pair for each direction. Observations were deduplicated by service date, trip, and stop sequence. Headways use actual arrival timestamps partitioned by service date, route, direction, and stop; arrivals in the opposite direction never count. Scheduled-arrival counts come from the GTFS schedule active on each date. Gaps longer than four hours and gaps beginning before 5 AM were excluded from the article summary. The four-day “hit rates” were selected after looking at these same four days, so they are descriptive, not a forecast. An unobserved arrival can represent dropped service, a short turn, severe lateness, or a real-time-data failure. The rider-facing fact is the same narrower one: no bus was observed at that stop in that direction during the stated interval.
